“Baby sister, I was born game and I intend to go out that way.”— Rooster Cogburn, True Grit

“How are you still in pursuit? He’s barefoot in a rainforest!” shouts the radio of the man chasing after me. I can barely hear a slurred response. “HA, sole!” I say through a grin. That ugly guard must be nursing the jaw I rearranged for him. I don’t even need to know what they have to say, I’m not stupid. I heard those beeps, I saw their reaction, they’re gonna take me away. Well, not if I have anything to say about it.

I know no one on this island is going to stick up for me. Double rations in exchange for a teuila, a child without true lineage, that’s easy math. There are only two people in this whole world who look after me, Auntie Fia, and Jesus Himself. Even my own congregation was getting tired of feeding me. I guess I can’t blame them, Auntie Fia never said so but I knew she wasn’t going to be able to keep all of me fed. And I wasn’t about to let her start giving me her rations. At sixteen, I’m already bigger than most men on this island, which is useful for a couple things: shaking down coconut trees for their fruit, and making people think twice before picking a fight with me. But it takes a lot of coconuts to keep these pecs rippling. Guess that’s why everyone calls me popo, or coconut.

I crash through the night, starting with a curtain of broad leaves. I nearly eat dirt when my foot catches a root. I recover with all the grace of a sack of taro tumbling down a hill. There’s no point running straight ahead. I’ll run into the ocean eventually, and I’m not about to make the mistake of letting these men push me into a corner. I’ve seen enough Westerns to know that a cornered man has two options: surrender or shoot his way out.

Unfortunately, I have no gun. I also have no shoes. And I’m pretty sure I’ve never seen John Wayne running barefoot through a rainforest high stepping tree roots.

I slow down and listen. In the distance I can hear 3, maybe 4 of them?

“O lau afioga o le sulu lea…” I recite prayerfully under my breath, opening my eyes wide to take in whatever moonlight is able to push its way through the canopy above. Lord, I could use one of those lamps under my feet right now. I’ve walked this path a thousand times, but not usually in the dark. I know where the ground drops unexpectedly, where the roots knot together, and where the streams cut through the undergrowth. I also know where I can find food. My stomach chooses this moment to growl. “Not now.” I tell it. It growls again. “Traitor.” I say back.

I push through a stand of ferns and spot a flash of yellow among the leaves. Paogo fruit. Several have fallen from the tree and aren’t too mushy yet. I scoop one up, squeeze it, and wrinkle my nose. Not exactly a feast. But then, I’ve never been a picky eater. I twist at the outer rind and get to the mushy core, popping it in my mouth and sucking on it between breaths.

I reach a narrow section of the trail where young saplings grow thick beneath the larger trees. I grab a couple of them nearest the trail and bend them, testing an idea. I look around, searching for something sturdy enough to use as a cord. Vines hang everywhere, some thick enough to support my weight. As I start to tie a knot my hands are slippery from the juice of the paogo. Maybe now wasn’t the time for a snack.

I secure the vine to the bent saplings, a crude spring trap that should hopefully ruin someone’s evening and not kill them. I pluck the vine like a guitar string to test it. Perfect. I duck behind a tree and wait.

The first guard appears between the trunks, his face shiny with sweat. He’s carrying some kind of weapon in both hands and looking around like the forest is out to get him. Yeah, this time it is. He passes the trigger spot. I pull. The saplings slap back across the trail and catch him square in the chest. He hits the ground with a loud thud.

I clap a hand over my mouth. Oh, that was beautiful. His weapon clatters into the leaves. He scrambles up onto his side, cursing, while another guard barrels into him from behind, stepping on his head with a heavy boot.

I bite the inside of my cheek so hard I nearly draw blood as I take off in the opposite direction trying not to laugh out loud.

The forest drops toward a shallow stream. I splash through it, then climb the other side, stepping onto exposed roots to avoid leaving footprints in the mud. I squeeze between two palm trees, scraping my shoulders, and barely stop in time before a deep hole in the ground. I know this place.

I drag a few branches across the hole and scatter leaves over it. It’s not a proper pit trap, like the ones I’ve seen in movies, but it doesn’t need to be. A man running in the dark will do most of the work himself. I arrange the branches so they look like the surrounding forest floor. Then I step back and admire my handiwork.

A shout comes from behind me. How are they still tracking me? I think back and remember the tech those guys in gray were carrying with them. Must have some fancy scanners. I scramble up the ridge as it begins to rain lightly. My feet hurt heaps now. Every twig feels like a nail, and I’ve got a thorn in the ball of my left foot that I’m trying very hard not to think about. And I’m still hungry. This blows.

I hear the guards getting closer.

“Where did he go?”

“How should I know?”

“He was right here!”

“Then why are you asking me?”

I press my back against a tree and close my eyes.

“Lord, if you could give me a little more time, I’d appreciate it? I promise I’ll stop complaining about the food at church, ay?” I whisper.

A sharp crack comes from below, followed by a thud and a string of words that would get a man barred from the pulpit. I peek over the ridge. Both guards have fallen into the mud at the bottom of the pit, one of them trying to pull out the other who got stuck face down. I have to put my fist in my mouth. This is better than TV.

Moving downhill the forest gets thinner. I know the coast is just a little further to the east, and I know there are fishing boats that leave before dawn. If I can reach the dock without being spotted, I might be able to hide aboard one of those boats until the guards give up. It’s not a great plan. But it’s a plan.

I reach the edge of a clearing and stop beneath the shadow of a breadfruit tree. Ahead, the ground slopes down toward a cluster of houses. A few lights are still on, but most of the village is dark. Beyond them I can see moonlight reflecting on the water.

There. The fishing boats. I can see their masts against the sky, I’m almost there.

I look down at my feet. They’re bleeding in two places, and I can feel every stone I’ve stepped on. My legs ache and my shirt is soaked with sweat. Bet the ladies would still say wassup.

I start moving again, keeping low. I avoid the open ground, stepping from shadow to shadow. There’s a narrow path between the last row of houses and the beach. If I can reach it, I can disappear among the boats and wait for dawn. I can almost feel the sea breeze on my face.

Then I hear the radio.

“Visual confirmed. Target moving east.”

I freeze. The voice is coming from somewhere ahead. Not behind me. Ahead. I squint and see that a figure stands between me and the dock. He doesn’t have a weapon though, he’s just standing there, hands behind his back. I plant my feet.

I’m tired. I’m bleeding. I’m barefoot in the middle of the night, and I haven’t eaten properly in two days. But I’m still standing.

“Ia tau ia matua tau!” I yell, picking up a fallen branch like a club.

What would Rooster Cogburn say right now?

“Fill your hand, you son of a —”

Something sharp hits me in the back.

For a second, I don’t understand what’s happened. A cold numbness spreads to my neck and outward. My fingers loosen around the branch. I turn. A fourth guard stands behind me, holding a long, narrow device. A thin wisp of vapor curls from its end.

I stare at him.

“That,” I say, “is cheating.”

My knees buckle. I try to catch myself, but my arms don’t seem interested in taking orders. The ground rises toward me, and I hit it hard enough to knock the breath from my lungs.

Boots approach through the dirt.

Someone kicks the branch out of my reach.

“Secure him.”

Hands grab my arms. Something tight closes around my wrists. I try to fight. But my arms have turned into wet rope, and the world is tilting now.

The broken-jaw guard crouches beside me.

“You’re quite a runner,” he mumbles, while slamming my head into the ground.

I want to tell him that I’m a whole lot of things. A good cook, for example. A fine dancer, when the occasion demands it. A man who has never lost an argument with a rooster. But my tongue feels fat and I decide it’s not worth it.

The guard says something else, but the words blur together. I feel myself being lifted, my feet dragging through the leaves. Someone complains about my weight. I would laugh if I could. The world dims at the edges.

I remember a scripture Auntie Fia made me memorize.

“…lie down unto the Lord, that he may watch over you in your sleep; and when thou risest in the morning let thy heart be full of thanks unto God” I say to a confused-looking guard as everything goes black.

End of Chapter Three
